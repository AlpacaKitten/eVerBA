  \chapter{Introduction}  
 
In theory, we have a number of cryptographic protocols that can be proven secure mathematically under various assumptions and conditions \cite{Dowling2021TLS13}\cite{OneTimePad}. They are commonly in use and enable us to communicate between two or more parties securely. However, even if a protocol is secure, we cannot rely on that alone to claim a whole system secure. Multiple angles exist that attack the environment in which the protocol is implemented by analyzing the cache \cite{AESSidechannel}, measuring power consumption \cite{EisenbarthPowerAnalysis}\cite{RandolphPowerAnalysis}, or measuring the time an algorithm needs to recover the secret \cite{KocherPowerAttacks}. Attack vectors like these are known as \textit{side channels}, and they open up a wide range of possible leakages and security breaks that are difficult to anticipate through cryptanalysis of the algorithm alone \cite{KocherPowerAttacks}. One of the hardest classes of side-channel attacks to mitigate is the case where the attacker has physical possession of the device performing the computation\cite{AndersonTamperResistance}. In this setting, it is critical to secure each individual step of the calculation, since even brief, localized leakage at any single step can allow a malicious party to reconstruct secret data that the algorithm itself never exposes\cite{IshaiCircuits}.

To secure each step, the most used countermeasure is masking \cite{BerndtBuyOneGetOneForFree}: Each sensitive value is split into multiple values called \emph{shares} in such a way that a sufficiently high number of shares is needed to reconstruct the secret. The algorithms used for calculating on shares securely are called \emph{gadgets}. They build the foundation and can be built in hardware. They are constructed in such a way that it is possible to verify security, either using standard cryptographical approaches, \ie handwritten proofs, or automated verifying tools.


As always, the tension between the need for security and the need for efficient computations leads to interesting problems: how do we design gadgets in such a way that all security standards can be upheld while still keeping the computation time of the gadgets to a reasonable limit?


\section{Scope and Contributions}
In this thesis, the LaOla-multiplication gadget \cite{BerndtBuyOneGetOneForFree} used for multiplying two polynomially masked secrets will be looked at. I designed two more efficient variants of the original gadget:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The first variant, \textbf{LaOlaSquare}, allows us to square a polynomially masked secret without the costs of a full LaOla-Operation. Instead of two calls of SplitRed on the same masking, SplitRed is only called once. To hide the secret during the multiplication, a Zero-Encoding is split into two parts using a simplified version of the SplitRed-Algorithm. We then add the split parts of the secret onto it, creating the second part for our multiplication. Both parts can now be multiplied securely, and although the addition of the SplitRed-Variant introduces more complexity to the overall gadget, we get a huge efficiency boost out of it.
  \item The second variant, \textbf{LaOlaLin}, builds on the first one. It adds a linear function in the form of $a\cdot x+b$, so that for $y=ax+b$, $x\cdot y$ can be calculated quite easily. For this, we follow the same procedure as we did for LaolaSquare, but in addition multiply both halves of the second part by $a$ and add $b$ onto one of them. 
  \item Furthermore, I found an improvement in the original SplitRed-Algorithm, which improves its complexity and runtime significantly and therefore improves both of the LaOla variants as well as the original gadget.
\end{itemize}
 Most of this work was done using \toolname{eVer} \cite{eVer2026}, a framework for verifying gadgets automatically. Every finding was implemented and tested there. 

I also extended \toolname{eVer} by implementing an algorithm for additive sharing that was introduced by Coron \etal in 2015 \cite{Coron2015}.

\chapter{Background}
Suppose you have a circuit where two values $a$ and $b$ get multiplied resulting in a value $c$, where $a$ and $b$ have to remain secret. This secrecy can be in danger if an attacker has access to the circuit, as they could just measure the electricity going through specific wires in the circuit and draw conclusions about $a$ and $b$ from that. By measuring power an attacker can reveal the sequence of instructions executed and depending on the protocol in use gain information about the secret from there. Because a chip's power draw depends on the data bits it's processing, an attacker measuring many power traces during encryption and statistically correlating them with guesses about the secret reveal the key bit by bit, since the correct key guess produces a recognizable power profile \cite{DiffPowerAnalysis}.
Those attack vectors are called \emph{probing side-channel-attacks}. We have to find ways to keep our values a secret, even when a malicious intruder is allowed to read them.

This is where \textit{masking} and \textit{gadget sharing} come into play. Instead of multiplying $a$ and $b$ directly, we will mask them by splitting them up into multiple parts called \textit{shares}, through a procedure called secret sharing \cite{shamirsecret}. Those shares are constructed in such a way that if $n$ shares exist, if we know $t$ with $t<n$ of the shares, nothing about the secret gets revealed. By processing the shares individually, once the calculation takes place, none of the secret values are ever processed in their raw form. This way, no single intermediate computation will leak any parts of the secret, so even when a malicious party has access to $t$ of the shares, as long as $t<n$, they won't be able to conclude anything about the secret and the combined value an encoding.
Encodings are symbolized by letters in \textbf{bold} font, i.e., \textbf{x} is an encoding of the secret $x$. Our goal is to achieve security, even when some of the shares get leaked.
    
\begin{definition}[Shamir's Secret Sharing]\cite{shamirsecret}
  Shamir's secret sharing is a way to divide data into $k$ pieces so that you need at least $n$ of them to reconstruct the data, but even complete knowledge of $t=n-1$ pieces reveals nothing at all about the data. 
    \end{definition}


  \section{Gadget Sharing}
  \label{ch:background}
   
    For our purposes, instead of relying on a vague notion of security, we need a more formal definition to compare our gadgets against. Specifically, we want our gadgets to show strong non-interference-characteristics:
    \begin{definition}[$t$-(S)NI]\cite{tSNIDef} A gadget G is $t$- \textit{Strong Non Interfering} if for any set of $t_1$ probes on its intermediate values and every set of $t_2$ probes on its output shares with $t_1+t_2 \leq t$, the totality of the probes can be simulated with $t_1$ shares of each input.


    \end{definition}
    In simple terms, a gadget is $t$-SNI if for every possible combination of $t$ or less output-values and intermediate values that might leak during the calculation, we still learn nothing about the actual secret values being calculated. To an attacker, it is indistinguishable from randomness.

    There are two main methods to mask secrets: the simplest approach is splitting $a$ into $a_1,\dots,a_n$, by sampling independent uniformly distributed (\iud) values $a_1,...,a_{n-1}$ from our field $\mathbb{F}$ and choose $a_n$ in such a way that $a_1 \oplus ... \oplus a_n=a$, where $\oplus$ is the addition of $\mathbb{F}$. If we now take any subset of $n-1$ shares, we are not able to reconstruct our original value; in fact, we learn nothing at all about it. For $n=2$, this is equal to the classical one-time-pad we know from symmetric cryptography. This concept is known as additive Boolean masking, because we add the shares together to get our value.
    Instead of a simple multiplication $a \cdot b =c$, we now multiply the shares individually and compute a new set of sharings, such that when we XOR all the shares together, we receive the
    same value as our simple, insecure multiplication we would have done directly on the secret: $\mathbf{a} \cdot_G \mathbf{b}  = \Bigl(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n} a_i\Bigr) \cdot \Bigl(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n} b_i\Bigr)  = \bigoplus_{i=1}^{n} c_i  = \mathbf{c}$. Note that the shares cannot simply be multiplied locally, i.e., in general $c_i \neq a_i \cdot b_i$, since this would ignore the cross terms $a_i \cdot b_j$ with $i \neq j$.
    The disadvantage of using additive sharing lies in its inability to handle faults. As soon as we introduce a singe fault, the value itself gets changed. So if we assume an active attacker model, we cannot guarantee security and correctness of the calculation.


    \section{Polynomial Gadget Sharing}
    With polynomial gadget sharing, instead of splitting a value into multiple shares that get added together to receive our secret, we instead build a polynomial of degree $d$. We 
    take our secret values $s$ and embed them as the first coefficient of the polynomial, then we draw $k-1$ random values and obtain the poliynomial $\mathbf{A}= s + r_1x^1+r_2 x^2 +....+r_kx^k$. We then interpolate the polynomial at $n$ distinct points, from which our shares are derived.
    To accurately reconstruct a secret from a polynomial of degree $d$, one needs to know at least $d+1$ points on the polynomial, which we denote \textit{support points}. Since the masking relies on the degree, which is independent of the number of shares, it is also possible to reconstruct a secret with any subset of shares, as long as enough of them are known, because recovering the secret requires interpolating the polynomial from at least $d+1$ shares ( see\ref{lm:polynomialInterpolation}), which we can evaluate at the support point $0$. This differs to additive Boolean sharing, where we need to know all the shares to reconstruct the secret. Furthermore, as long as the number of faults is sufficiently small, using polynomial masking allows us to handle faults, since we are no longer bound by the need to use all shares to retrieve the secret. 


    \subsection{A Look at the Mathematical Foundations}
    Let's take a look at the way we can do calculations and reconstruct the secret with such a polynomial. This section is based on chapter 5.3 of Berndt's Script for his lecture on advanced Cryptology (\cite{SkriptBerndt}). Assume we have a secret value $s$ we want to hide and constructed the polynomial as described above. Let the polynomial be  $\mathbf{A}(x)= a_0 + a_1x^1+a_2 x^2 +....+a_kx^k=\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_ix^i$. 

    \begin{lemma}[Polynomial interpolation]\cite{BerndtBuyOneGetOneForFree}
      \label{lm:polynomialInterpolation}
    Every polynomial \textbf{f} of degree at most $d$ can be interpolated from $d+1$ distinct point-value pairs $(\alpha_i,\mathbf{f}(\alpha_i))$

    \end{lemma}
    
    If the polynomial is known to us, we can now simply receive a corresponding value to any given point $\alpha$ by calculating $\mathbf{A}(\alpha)$. For many support points $\alpha_0,...,\alpha_n$, we can easily write this as a matrix:
    \[\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha_0^0 & \alpha_0^1 & ... & \alpha_0^t\\
\alpha_1^0 & \alpha_1^1 & ... & \alpha_1^t\\
     &...       &     &\\
\alpha_n^0 & \alpha_n^1 & ... & \alpha_n^t
\end{pmatrix} \cdot(a_0,a_1,...,a_t)^T=(\mathbf{A}(\alpha_0),\mathbf{A}(\alpha_1),...,\mathbf{A}(\alpha_n))^T\]
  The matrix shown above is called a Vandermonde matrix and will be called $V_{\symbfit{\alpha}}$. This Matrix is invertible if $\alpha_i \neq \alpha_j; \forall i \neq j$ , so we can build $V_{\symbfit{\alpha}}^{-1}$ so that for $n=t+1$, it holds that$ V^{-1}_{\symbfit{\alpha}}(\mathbf{A}(\alpha_0),\mathbf{A}(\alpha_1),...,\mathbf{A}(\alpha_{t+1}))=(a_0,...,a_t)^T  $
  Note that we can use any of the support points here, as long as we use enough of them. The calculation shown above shows us, that we can reconstruct the polynomial if we only know enough support points to construct $V^{-1}_{\symbfit{\alpha}}$. If we assume our secret to be stored in $a_0$ and if we write $(\lambda_{1,i-1},...\lambda_{n,i-1})$ to denote the $i$-th row of $V^{-1}_{\symbfit{\alpha}}$, we can show that $a_0=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{\symbfit{\alpha},i} \mathbf{A}(\alpha_i)$. So for our calculations, we can reconstruct the secret entirely based on the points and no not necessarily need the polynomial itself. 
  
  A different view to access the secret is via the Lagrange polynomial.
  \begin{definition}[Lagrange Polynomial]\cite{SkriptBerndt}
    A Lagrange polynomial is defined as \[ L_{\alpha}^{(i)}(x)=\frac{\prod_{j \neq i}(x-\alpha_j)}{\prod_{j \neq i}(\alpha_i-\alpha_j)}\]
  where $\alpha_i$ denotes the $i$-th support point. 
  \end{definition}
  We can now build our polynomial \textbf{A} by building the Lagrange Polynomial for every support point and multiplying it with the corresponding value of \textbf{A} at the current support point:
  \[ \textbf{B}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{\alpha}^{(i)}(x)\textbf{A}(\alpha_i)=\textbf{A}(x)\]
  The equality between \textbf{B} and \textbf{A} gets clear once we realize that both polynomials share $t$ value-pairs. As soon as our degree is smaller than $t$, they have to be identical. This way of viewing the polynomial can be quite helpful, since it allows us to reconstruct the original polynomial once we know enough support points.
  This representation gives us the polynomial only using the support points themselves. Using this, we can now also write $\lambda_{\alpha,i}= L_{\alpha}^{(i)}(0)$.

  \section{The LaOla Gadget}
  One problem we encounter when masking secrets via polynomials arises once we want to multiply two masked secrets. Let \textit{Dec}(\textbf{S}) denote the secret $s$ encoded by the polynomial \textbf{S}. When simply adding two polynomially masked secrets, we can just achieve that by adding shares locally, \ie if $\mathbf{F}$ and $\mathbf{G}$ are polynomials of degree $d$ with $\text{\textit{Dec}(\textbf{F)}}=\mathbf{F}(0)=f$ and $\text{\textit{Dec}(\textbf{G)}}=\mathbf{G}(0)=g$, every party computes $\mathbf{H}(x_i) = \mathbf{F}(x_i) + \mathbf{G}(x_i)$. Since addition is linear, $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{F} + \mathbf{G}$ is again a polynomial of degree at most $d$ with $\text{\textit{Dec}(\textbf{H)}}=\mathbf{H}(0) = h = f + g$. The same $d+1$ support points therefore still suffice to decode the secret, and the shares of $\mathbf{H}$ form a valid sharing of $f + g$.
  However, when dealing with multiplication, problems arise. The obvious approach is to transfer the methods used for addition, in that every party computes $\mathbf{H}(x_i) = \mathbf{F}(x_i) \cdot \mathbf{G}(x_i)$ at its own support point $x_i$ does not suffice. Although the calculated values are indeed points of the product polynomial $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{G}$ with $\mathbf{H}(0) = f \cdot g$, this local approach causes problems: The polynomial $\mathbf{H}$ has degree $2d$, so we need $2d+1$ support points to decode the secret instead of $d+1$. The number of shares we needed beforehand is no longer enough, and every further multiplication doubles the degree again, so we would need more and more support points.
  Even with enough points, The coefficients of $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{G}$ are all functions of the same $2d$ random values, so $\mathbf{H}$ is not a uniformly random degree-$2d$ polylnomial with $\mathbf{H}(0) = fg$. Already a single share $\mathbf{H}(x_i) = \mathbf{F}(x_i) \cdot \mathbf{G}(x_i)$ is a product of two independent uniform values and therefore not uniform. Locally multiplying does therefore not produce a fresh sharing of $fg$, but one that stays correlated with the sharings of $f$ and $g$.

  Over the years, different versions to deal with this problem have been tested. In this thesis, I will focus on one of them in particular: \textbf{LaOla}.
    LaOla is a gadget built by Berndt \etal, it takes two polynomial maskings $\mathbf{F}$ and $\mathbf{G}$ as input and outputs $\mathbf{F}\cdot \mathbf{G}$. It was first introduced in TODO. The core concept revolves around splitting the polynomials into two parts before multiplying them together to mask the secrets:
    \[ \mathbf{F}\cdot \mathbf{G}=(\mathbf{F}'+\mathbf{F}'')\cdot(\mathbf{G}'+\mathbf{G}'')= \mathbf{F}'\mathbf{G}'+\mathbf{F}'\mathbf{G}''+\mathbf{F}''\mathbf{G}'+\mathbf{F}''\mathbf{G}''\] 
    To split the encodings, the SplitRed algorithm is used. This algorithm will be explained in Section \ref{sec:splitred} and uses a neat little trick: the two halves of the split polynomial both individually have degree $d$, but parts of the arguments get chosen inverse to each other, so that once they are added together, the resulting polynomial has degree $d/2$. This way, the increase in degree gets subverted, so that in the end we have a polynomial $\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{F}\cdot \mathbf{G}$ with \textit{deg}(\textbf{H})=$d$. 

    Once the polynomials have been split, the smaller parts get calculated before adding them all together. The full pseudocode for LaOla can be found in \ref{alg:laolamult} and shows the structure of the algorithm calls.


 \begin{Pseudocode}[caption={LaOlaMult as shown in \cite{eVer2026}}, label={alg:laolamult}]
    $\mathbf{Input}$: Encodings $\mathbf{F}$ and $\mathbf{G}$
    $\mathbf{Output}$: Encoding $\mathbf{Q}$ so that $Dec(\mathbf{Q})=Dec(\mathbf{F})\cdot Dec(\mathbf{G})$

    $\mathbf{F}', \mathbf{F}''$ <- SplitRed($\mathbf{F}, \lceil d/2 \rceil$)
    $\mathbf{G}', \mathbf{G}''$ <- SplitRed($\mathbf{G}, \lfloor d/2 \rfloor$)
    $\mathbf{H}^{(0)}$<-$ SWMult(\mathbf{F}', \mathbf{G}')$
    $\mathbf{H}^{(1)}$<-$ SWMult(\mathbf{F}', \mathbf{G}'')$
    $\mathbf{H}^{(2)}$<-$ SWMult(\mathbf{F}'', \mathbf{G}')$
    $\mathbf{H}^{(3)}$<-$ SWMult(\mathbf{F}'', \mathbf{G}'')$
    $\mathbf{Q}$<-$SWComp(\mathbf{H}^{(0)},\mathbf{H}^{(1)},\mathbf{H}^{(2)},\mathbf{H}^{(3)}$)
    return $\mathbf{Q}$
    \end{Pseudocode}


    Since the secrets from \textbf{F} and \textbf{G} are each split across two parts which never get calculated together openly, there is no calculation in which a whole secret is leaked.
    The attacker would have to listen to at least two of the multiplications to learn anything about the whole secret, and since both parts resulting from the SplitRed call also 
    have a degree of $d$, we achieve $d$-SNI.

    \section{The Splitred-Algorithm}
    \label{sec:splitred}
    The SplitRed-Algorithm is the core algorithm I tackled in this thesis, so I will make some room here to explain it properly. Originally it was used in the LaOla multiplication gadget.
    Although originally introduced in TODO, since I am working in the \toolname{eVer} framework a lot, I will use the version of the algorithm provided by Gourjon, Orlt, Pauls and Treff in their paper on the framework \cite{eVer2026}.
    The general idea of the algorithm is to take one polynomial encoding $\mathbf{F}$ of degree $d$ and split it into two polynomial encodings of degree $d$. The two resulting polynomials, if added together, mask the same secret as $\mathbf{F}$, but this addition leads to a degree decrease, 
    the degree of the resulting polynomial halves. The degree reduction is achieved by designing the two split polynomials in such a way that the last $d/2$ coefficients are inverse to each other so that they cancel out when added together. This is neat, because if we multiply two polynomials of degree $d/2$, the resulting polynomial will once again have a degree of $d$. 
    We can write $\mathbf{F}$ as $\mathbf{F}'+\mathbf{F}''$, since both sides of the equation mask the same secret. To give an example, let \textbf{F} encode the secret $f$ 

    Let's take a look at how the algorithm achieves the desired properties (masking the same secret ad \textbf{F} while halving the degree when \textbf{F}' and \textbf{F}'' are added together) in more detail:

 \begin{Pseudocode}[caption={SplitRed for $n=d+e+1$ and $k\leq \lfloor \frac{d}{2} \rfloor$ as shown in \cite{eVer2026}}, label={alg:splitred}]
    $\mathbf{Input}$: Encoding $\mathbf{F}$ of degree d and Target degree d'
  $\mathbf{Output}$: Encodings $\mathbf{F'}$, $\mathbf{F''}$ so that Dec(SWAdd($\mathbf{F'}$, $\mathbf{F''}$))= Dec($\mathbf{F}$) 
    and Deg(SWAdd($\mathbf{F'}$, $\mathbf{F''}$))=d'

    if d'=d then 
      return $\mathbf{F},\mathbf{0}$
    for j $\in \lceil n/2 \rceil$ do
      $ d_{enc}$ <- d - j if j < d+1-k else d
      $\hat{\mathbf{G}}^j$ <- $opt\mbox{-}ZEnc^{d_{enc}}_n$
    for j $\in \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ do
      $ \mathbf{G}^j$ <- $\hat{ \mathbf{G}}^j$
    if n mod 2 = 1 then 
      $ \mathbf{G}^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor -1}$ <- SWAdd($ \mathbf{G}^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor -1},\hat{ \mathbf{G}}^{\lceil n/2 \rceil -1}$)
    for j $\in \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ do
      for i $\in \lbrack n \rbrack$ do
        $\mathcal{F}_i'^j$<- $\hat{\lambda}^i_j \cdot F_j$
      $\mathcal{F}^j$ <- SWAdd$(\mathcal{F}'^j, \mathbf{G}^j)$
      $\mathbf{F}'$ <- SWAdd$(\mathbf{F}', \mathcal{F}^j)$
    for j $\in \lceil n/2 \rceil$ do
      $ d'_{enc}$ <- d' - j if j < d'+1-k else d'
      $\mathbf{G}^{j+\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$<- SWSub($optZEnc_n^{d'_{enc}}, \hat{\mathbf{G}}^j$)
      for i $\in \lbrack n \rbrack$ do 
        $\mathcal{F}_i'^{j+\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$ <- $\hat{\lambda}^i_{j+ \lfloor n/2 \rfloor} \cdot F_{j+ \lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$
      $\mathcal{F}^{j+\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$ <- SWAdd ( $\mathcal{F}'^{j+\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$, $\mathbf{G}^{j+\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$)
      $\mathbf{F}'' $<- SWAdd($\mathbf{F}'', \mathcal{F}^{j+\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$)
    
    return $\mathbf{F}$', $\mathbf{F}''$
    \end{Pseudocode}
   

    The algorithm takes a polynomial encoding $\mathbf{F}$ of degree $d$. \textbf{F} will encode $e$ secrets and consist of $n=d+e+1$ shares.
     
    We first draw $\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil$ encodings of the secret zero, denoted ZEnc, which we then sum up. Each ZEnc has a degree of $d-j$ if $j<d+1-k$ or the full degree of $d$ else. This leads us to a triangular shape as seen in Fig.\ref{fig:G_hat}. The case distinction assures us that the degree will not be negative.
    If $n$ is uneven, we need a special case for adding two ZEncs onto one value.

    We want to split the secret between the two split polynomials. To do this, we take the $j$-th share $F_j$ of the input encoding \textbf{F}. We scale it by $\hat{\lambda}_i$, where $\hat{\lambda}$ is used for the interpolation of the polynomial and given beforehand. 
    We then take the $i$-th sharing of the $j$-th ZEnc saved in $\mathbf{G}$ and add them onto our scaled secrets. In the end, we add them all together to form $\mathbf{F}'$. Because we only stay inside this loop until $j$ reaches $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$, only the first $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ shares of \textbf{F} get used to form $\mathbf{F}'$.

    We then draw $d'$ ZEncs for a smaller triangle we need for \textbf{F}''.  The remaining values get chosen inverse to the ones we used in \textbf{F}'' by simply subtracting $\hat{\mathbf{G}}$  
    together the terms can cancel out.
    This allows us to receive the needed degree-reduction mentioned earlier. We then follow the same procedure as before to integrate the missing parts of our original secret into $\mathbf{F}''$.

    \section{\toolname{eVer}}
    The tool \toolname{eVer} is the main framework I worked with for this thesis. It's a python tool developed by Gourjon \etal and used to verify security of gadgets.
    It is not easy to prove a gadget as secure, with the best solution often being a brute-force approach where all possible combinations of leakage get tested. This tool implements exactly that, it is a way to automatically verify side-channel security by trying out all possible tuples of leakages.Verifying a gadget as e.g., $t$-NI requires checking whether the $t$-NI notion hold for all possible $\binom{|O|}{t}$, where $O$ denotes the set of all possible adversarial observations. The number of tuples can also be used as a first estimate for the complexity of the gadget, since more tuples needing to be tested arise for more complex gadgets. The aim for this thesis was therefore to implement new gadgets that can be verified by \toolname{eVer} while reducing the number of tuples needing to be checked. A big advantage of \toolname{eVer} lays in its ability to check gadgets for $t$-SNI and handle poly-masking gadgets.
    Since \toolname{eVer} checks every tuple fully automatically, it is especially useful when wanting to test if small changes made to an existing gadget still hold security.

  \chapter{Implementing a New Gadget for Additive Sharing}
  \label{ch:additive}

  To get more familiar with \toolname{eVer} as well as masking gadgets in general, I implemented an additive sharing gadget. Specifically, I implemented a gadget introduced in \cite{Coron2015}. This gadget (as shown in Alg. \ref{alg:additivesharing}) is derived from a masking scheme designed by Ishai, Sahai and Wagner in \cite{IshaiCircuits} called \emph{ISW scheme}. It was adapted in such a way that instead of processing values directly, for every share $a$ multiplications adapt the form $a \cdot g(a)$ with $g$ being a linear function.



 \begin{Pseudocode}[caption={Secure Evaluation of $h:x \odot g(x)$ for additive sharing}, label={alg:additivesharing}]
$\mathbf{Input:}$ shares $(a_i)_i$ such that $\sum_{i} a_i =a$
$\mathbf{Output:}$ shares $c_i$ satisfying $\sum_i c_i = a \odot g(a)$ $\text{\textit{for}}$ some $\mathbb{F}_2$-linear $\text{\textit{function}}$ $g$

for i=0 to d do 
  for j=i+1 to d do
    $r_{i,j} \rightarrow ^{\$} \mathbb{F}_{2^n}$
    $r'_{i,j} \rightarrow ^{\$} \mathbb{F}_{2^n}$
    t<-$r_{i,j} \oplus a_i \odot g(r'_{i,j})$
    t<-t $\oplus (r'_{i,j}\odot g(a_i))$
    t<-t $\oplus (a_i \odot g(a_j\oplus r'_{i,j}))$
    t<-t $\oplus ((a_j \oplus r'_{i,j})\odot g(a_i))$
    $r_{i,j}$<-t
  end for
end for
for i=0 to d do
    $c_i$ <- $a_i \odot g(a_i)$
    for j=0 to d, $j\neq i$ do $c_i$ <- $c_i \oplus r_{i,j}$
end for
    \end{Pseudocode}

   

This code calculates $a \cdot g(a)$, where $a$ denotes an additively masked secret and $g$ is a linear function. When multiplying two masked secrets, we have to multiply each share with each share. We are able to multiply a sharing with itself without problems, but once we start multiplying different shares to the same secret, we reveal too much information and break $d$-SNI. For this reason, we add randomness onto the cross terms in such a way that we can safely multiply them together. We define $r$ to be symmetric, so that $r_{i,j}=r_{j,i}$.

    \section{A Squaring-Gadget}

    The most expensive part about LaOla is the SplitRed-call. When a masked value is squared, e.g. $\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{F}$, SplitRed is called twice with the same input.
    Taking a look at the calculations themselves, here is what happens:
    \[\mathbf{F}² = \mathbf{F} \times \mathbf{F} = (\mathbf{F'} +\mathbf{F''})(\mathbf{F'''}+\mathbf{F}^{IV}) = \mathbf{F'F'''} +\mathbf{F'F}^{IV} + \mathbf{F''F'''} + \mathbf{F''F}^{IV}.\]
    It is important that $\mathbf{F'} +\mathbf{F''}$ and $\mathbf{F'''}+\mathbf{F}^{IV}$ encode the same secret. The idea of this LaOla-variant rests on this fact, as such it is intuitive to remove one of the two SplitRed calls.
    If we take this approach at its most basic, we get \[\mathbf{F}²=(\mathbf{F'}+\mathbf{F''})²= \mathbf{F'}² +2\mathbf{F'}\mathbf{F''} + \mathbf{F''}².\] 
    Looking at the fact that we work over the field $\mathbb{F}_{2^8}$, the middle term cancels out, leaving us with $\mathbf{F'}² + \mathbf{F''}²$. We can calculate this securely, because none of the terms hides the full secret. In this case, we only need to call SplitRed once. 

    However, once a calculation is made on a different field, this no longer holds, and simply calculating $\mathbf{F'}\mathbf{F''}$ will break SNI, because we constructed them in such a way that the second half of each polynomial is dependent on the other one, leading dependent randomness can be observed. If we multiply $\mathbf{F'}$ and $\mathbf{F''}$, the whole secret we try to mask will get revealed, so we cannot calculate them plainly. \toolname{eVer} throws the error message shown in \ref{code:errormessage}, supporting this claim.

    \subsection{Conquering Dependency}
    I tried a few ways to tackle the problem of calculating the middle part while still keeping the whole algorithm secure. 

    The most brute-fore approach is to call a new standard LaOla-gadget on the two missing parts. Since LaOla is secure, this way also does not break SNI. However, at almost 3 times the original cost, this is horrendously expensive and in no way practical.
    I then tried a different approach. Instead of keeping $\mathbf{F'}$ and $\mathbf{F''}$ for the second term, the idea arose that we can add some extra randomness to refresh both terms cheaply and restore independent randomness without the full cost of a new SplitRed call.  
    First, I tried simply refreshing $\mathbf{F'}$ and $\mathbf{F''}$ by using the OptRefresh gadget shown in \cite{eVer2026} that is already implemented in \toolname{eVer}. This however was not enough to reach SNI, when trying this approach, \toolname{eVer} throws the following error-Message:

    It looks like \toolname{eVer} throws the error at a later tuple, which leads me to believe that the approach has potential to work. I then tried to take a closer look at SplitRed, and tried to find ways to simplify it for the second call that would happen in LaOla.
    \\
    If we split a zero-encoding which is then added onto the second part of the SplitRed polynomial, this should add enough randomness to guarantee security, because it would mask the known randomness with fresh one, making them undifferentiable to an attacker from one another.

    In the worst case scenario we would have the same costs as the original LaOla, but there seems to be opportunities to simplify SplitRed for the zero-encoding. The calculation 
    we want to make looks like this (where $\mathbf{Z}'$ and $\mathbf{Z''}$ symbolize a split polynomial that encodes a zero):
    \[\mathbf{F}²=(\mathbf{F'}+\mathbf{F''})(\mathbf{F'}+\mathbf{F''}+\mathbf{Z'}+\mathbf{Z''})= (\mathbf{F'}+\mathbf{F''})((\mathbf{F'}+\mathbf{Z'})+(\mathbf{F''}+\mathbf{Z''})).\]
    
  We can formalize this to get the whole code for the gadget and end up with the code as shown in Alg. \ref{alg:laolasquare}

\section{Adding a Linear Function}

  We now have a less expensive way to square a polynomial encrypted secret using LaOlaSquare. I then tried out a way to expand on those findings by adding a linear function to one of the splits. This was inspired by the additive sharing algorithm by Coron, Rouff et al. published in \cite{Coron2015} and that I implemented in Chapter \ref{ch:additive}. The idea is to interpret the second factor $\mathbf{G}$ as values in relation to $\mathbf{F}$, ergo finding a function $g$ where $g(\mathbf{F})=\mathbf{G}$.
  Taking a function $ax +b$, this version of LaOla allows us to calculate $x \cdot (ax+b)$, where $x$ is a secret encoded by \textbf{F}. The calculation looks as follows:
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{split}
    \mathbf{F}\cdot \mathbf{G}& =\mathbf{F}\cdot (a\cdot \mathbf{F}+b)\\
    & =(\mathbf{F'}+\mathbf{F''})\cdot (a\cdot(\mathbf{F'}+\mathbf{F''}+\mathbf{Z'}+\mathbf{Z''})+b) \\
    & = (\mathbf{F'}+\mathbf{F''})(a\cdot(\mathbf{F'}+\mathbf{Z'})+a\cdot(\mathbf{F''}+\mathbf{Z''})+b)\\
    \end{split}
  \end{equation*}

  The algorithm is called with $a$ and $b$ as additional parameters. We call SplitRed on \textbf{F}, call SplitRedZenc on a Zero-Encoding, calculate $\tilde{\mathbf{F}}'$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{F}}''$ as we did in LaOlaSquare and multiply $a$ onto both halves. We then add $b$ onto $\tilde{\mathbf{F}}'$. Now, the modified values of $\tilde{\mathbf{F}}'$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{F}}''$ represent our needed value $a \cdot \mathbf{F} + b$. We multiply all four parts together as usual.
  This version is cheaper than the original LaOla, but is restricted in its usefulness by the fact that we need to know \textbf{G} in relationship to \textbf{F}. In a real world implementation, this can be achieved via lookup tables quite easily.

\section{Using LaOla-Variants to Design AES S-Boxes}
AES, the advanced encryption standard, is a symmetric block cipher standardized by NIST in 2001 \cite{nist2001aes}. It iterates a round transformation composed of a key addition, a linear layer and a nonlinear layer which applies the same substitution-box (S-box) to every byte of the internal state\cite{ProvablySecureAESMasking}. This can be achieved by using masking schemes as for example shown in \cite{ProvablySecureAESMasking}. For this thesis, I used the LaOla-Square Gadget to implement a calculation of $x \rightarrow x^{254}$ as shown in Algorithm 4 of \cite{SBoxes}:

    $\mathbf{Require:}$ x 
    $\mathbf{Ensure: }y=x^{254}$ 
      y<-$x^2$                    (Type-I)
      x<- $x \times x^4$          (Type-II)

  \end{Pseudocode}

  Until the end of the thesis I was not able to implement the Type-II-calculation more efficiently. I therefore decided to use a workaround:
  
  \begin{Python}
   def power_of_five(self, g: PolyEnc, rc: count, mk_loc)-> LaolaMultGadget:
        f_square = LaolaSquareGadget(g,d,rc, mk_loc)
        self.randoms.update(f_square.randoms)
        f_four = LaolaSquareGadget(f_square, d, rc, mk_loc)
        self.randoms.update(f_four.randoms)
        f_five = LaolaMultGadget(g, f_four, d, rc, mk_loc)
        return f_five
  \end{Python}
  Now, it is quite easy to use the existing gadgets I implemented to realize the pseudocode. In total, The calculation needs to call LaOla 11 times, with 8 of them being the relatively inexpensive LaOlaSquare variant.


  \chapter{Conclusion and Outlook}
  We will now briefly summarize the contents and contributions of this work and outline some open problems regarding the topic.

  \subsection{Conclusion}
  This work did two things:
  
  for once, \toolname{eVer} was used as a tool for gadget security verification. It was expanded by the implementation of three more gadgets which can be used for further research, either by improving upon them or by comparing against them. The gadgets include a multiplication gadget for additive sharing \cite{Coron2015}, a variant of LaOla for squaring polynomial encodings and a variant of LaOla for multiplying two polynomial encodings using a linear function. Furthermore, an improved version of LaOla has also been implemented. As of September 2026, the paper introducing \toolname{eVer} has only been cited two times, so it is reasonable to assume that this Thesis can help with further improvement of the tool in addition to expanding the tool itself. 

  On the other hand, I improved LaOla and designed two more efficient variants for it that can be used in specific circumstances. By creating a second variant of SplitRed specifically designed for a secure split of a zero-encoding, I was able to implement a gadget that can square a polynomially masked secret. I also found a way to add a simple linear function to create s second variant that can be used to multiply two distinct secrets \textbf{F} and \textbf{G} for $\mathbf{G}=a\cdot\mathbf{F}+b$, which could be implemented with the help of lookup tables. Both of the variants do not violate $t$-strong-non-interference. They allow for LaOla to be used more, since the efficiency for those types of calculations is boosted. Furthermore, I found an improvement in the original SplitRed that allows us to Improve its efficiency by upt o 30\% (if we go by the number of tuples verified by \toolname{eVer})


  
  
  \subsection{Outlook}
  Looking into the future, there are some aspects where further research is required. 
  
  The new gadgets are not yet proven secure formally, and although \toolname{eVer} guarantees SNI, a formal mathematical proof of their security would be beneficial to understand why certain aspects are secure. In particular, it would be interesting to investigate why the new version of SplitRed holds against probing, even when some of the loops go through one less iteration.


  It would be interesting to take a deeper look into LaOla and research deeper into finding a gadget that, if given $x^n$, calculates $x^{n+1}$. Since this is a linear Operation in GF($2^8$), it is reasonable to assume that such an improvement might exist. In the last week of this thesis I was already investigating this question, but wasn't able to achieve useful results.

  In addition, it might be fun to look at other gadgets implemented in \toolname{eVer} and check them for improvements. Since \toolname{eVer} allows for an efficient workflow when it comes to trying out improvements and a lot of gadgets are already implemented, there is reason to believe that further improvements can be found. This could also happen using AI for an automated search. If found, many new opportunities for secure and efficient gadget design would arise.
